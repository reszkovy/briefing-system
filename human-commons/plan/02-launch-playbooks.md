# How comparable community/village projects actually got started

Research note. Confidence flagged per claim; unverified items marked **[thin]**.

---

## 1. Zuzalu (Montenegro, Mar–May 2023)

**Recruitment mechanism — the most copied model in this whole set.** Not a waitlist, not an open call. Roughly **a dozen "inviters"** were each given the right to invite people, who then completed an application form. Invitation graph first, form second. Vitalik seeded it from four distinct tribes deliberately: Ethereum/ZK researchers, longevity & biotech, European rationalists, and network-state/governance "meta" researchers. Cross-pollination between tribes was the product, not a side effect.

**Timeline:** January 2023 team of four scouting locations → February team of eight → **25 March 2023 opening with 200 residents**. Roughly **8–10 weeks from committed team to doors open.**

**Money:** Organisers bulk-rented ~100 apartments at once *and* picked off-season — that combination was the whole cost trick. Meals ran **$15/person/day** via a single local restaurant partnership. One resident reported **$800 covered an entire month**. Non-wealthy applicants with strong social ties were subsidised. Not a break-even commercial venture; a philanthropically-tinged experiment.

**Numbers:** 200 core residents, ~700 total through the door. No single nationality above one third. ~1/3 already digital nomads. Very few families with children.

**Single most cited success factor:** **two months is the threshold.** Vitalik's own claim is that a week-long conference produces contacts and a 10–20 person hacker house produces a clique, but two months of co-living produces collaborators.

**Most cited weakness:** opaque membership selection and undefined governance. Zuzalu never ran again in its original form — it forked into 20+ successor pop-ups within 18 months.

**Transferable lesson for a 20-person Italy 2027:** Appoint **5–8 named inviters**, each responsible for filling ~3 seats from a different discipline, and make the application a formality that follows the invite. Do not open a public waitlist for cohort one. Book off-season, block-book one building.

## 2. Edge City / Edge Esmeralda (Healdsburg CA, June 2024) — the successor that got the economics right

**Recruitment:** existing networks first — past Edge City attendees plus followers of Devon Zuegel's Esmeralda project — then organic word-of-mouth out of the SF Bay Area. A formal application existed, and **allied newsletters were given codes to skip the application** — i.e. trusted-referrer bypass rather than open access.

**Timeline:** Edge City ran its first village January 2024; Esmeralda followed in June 2024. Nine villages and ~12,000 cumulative attendees by 2026.

**Pricing — the clearest published tier structure in this set:**
| Segment | Price |
|---|---|
| Out-of-town, full month | ~$2,158 (range $595–$2,158 by length) + accommodation separately |
| Shorter stays | from $595 |
| **Sonoma County locals, full month** | **~$200** |

Dinner included for out-of-towners. Edge City is reported to have become **cash-flow positive** — the only pop-up in this study credibly described as such **[secondary source, Gitcoin case study; the organisers have not published a P&L]**.

**Numbers:** ~1,300 cycled through the month, ~200 stayed the full month, ~80 children, ages 6 weeks to 86 years. **93% of sessions were organised by attendees, not organisers.**

**Single most cited success factor:** the deliberate ~10x local discount bought genuine town integration rather than resentment — plus emergent programming, which removes the organiser's biggest cost line.

**Named failure mode:** late arrivals struggled to integrate because "the vibe had already been set."

**Transferable lesson:** Charge outsiders full freight and locals a token amount — treat the local subsidy as a marketing and licence-to-operate cost, not charity. Programme only ~10–20% of the schedule yourself; build the scaffolding for residents to fill the rest. Have everyone arrive **on the same day**.

## 3. Vitalia (Próspera, Roatán, Jan–Apr 2024)

**Recruitment:** self-selection from the longevity/biotech community, marketed openly. No invite gate of the Zuzalu kind.

**Timeline:** ran 6 Jan – 1 Mar 2024, extended to 1 April on demand.

**Pricing — unbundled, and this matters:** **$500 Access Pass** (events, breakfast, amenities) with **housing sold separately at $1,250–$4,250 per room per month** depending on quality and sharing. The low pass price plus market-rate housing is a materially different risk posture from Edge City's bundled ticket: the organiser doesn't carry empty-bed risk.

**Numbers:** 500+ visitors over 2.5 months; ~150 on the ground at any time; **40–50 chose to stay on afterwards** as residents, with a stated goal of 200 permanent.

**Single most cited factor for it working:** it did not build infrastructure — it rented an existing special economic zone. Próspera supplied the legal regime, buildings and utilities.

**And the corresponding risk:** total dependence on a politically contested host. Honduran government hostility to Próspera is an existential overhang, and Toby Shorin's field report notes "a slight air of condescension" from participants toward neighbouring Honduran communities — the local-relations failure Edge City avoided.

**Transferable lesson:** Sell the **programme** and the **bed** as separate line items. The programme fee should cover your fixed costs standing alone; then a half-full house is disappointing rather than fatal. Also: a residency's real success metric is how many people come back or stay, not how many attend.

## 4. Cabin (cabin.city) — the cautionary DAO

**Recruitment:** the smallest and most personal first cohort here. Jon Hillis funded it from his Instacart earnings on family-gifted land — a 28-acre property with a four-bedroom cabin in the Texas Hill Country, "Neighborhood Zero." The first gathering was **a friend plus about six others** during the 2020 pandemic exodus. Seven people, all personal invites.

**Timeline:** informal coliving 2020 → creator residencies → CabinDAO → 500+ ₡ABIN token holders → distributed "network city" of affiliated neighbourhoods → **DAO wound down in 2025 after roughly four years**, treasury distributed to token holders.

**Money:** creator residencies were subsidised/free to residents, funded by token treasury. Never a break-even hospitality business.

**What killed it — the single most cited factor:** **operational overhead exceeded what the network could sustain.** Governance, token mechanics and coordination consumed the energy that the physical places needed. The retrospective framing is "a proof of possibility," which is what you say when the thing didn't pay for itself.

**Transferable lesson:** Do not put a token, a DAO, or any governance apparatus anywhere near a 20-person test residency. Cabin's failure was not the cabin — it was everything bolted onto the cabin. One legal entity, one decision-maker, for at least the first three cohorts.

## 5. Culdesac Tempe — what the permanent version actually costs

**Recruitment:** conventional apartment leasing plus very heavy incentives — **4–6 weeks free rent, a $750/yr transit pass, a $1,000+ e-bike, ~$3,000/yr in mobility credits.** No curation, no application, no cohort. This is the control group: what happens when you build the place and skip the community-formation step.

**Timeline:** founded April 2018 (YC S18) → **opened November 2023. Five and a half years, idea to first resident.**

**Money:** $40M venture capital; the Tempe project itself ~$200M ($170M equity from Encore Capital). Rents $1,300 (studio) to $2,900 (3-bed).

**Lease-up:** **50+ residents at Nov 2023 launch → 225 by July 2024**, against 636 units and a 1,000-resident target. That is slow, and it is being bought with those incentives.

**Enabling condition:** Tempe granted a special parking exemption. Almost no other US city would have.

**Transferable lesson:** Capital-intensive permanence is a 5-year, nine-figure bet whose demand is still unproven after opening. A 20-person residency should **rent, not buy**, and should treat the 2027 cohort as demand discovery for a lease decision — never the reverse.

## 6. The Assemblage (NYC, 2017–2020)

**Recruitment:** membership sales into a wellness/coworking/coliving hybrid across three Manhattan locations, launched late 2017 at 114 E 25th St. Founder Rodrigo Niño came from Prodigy Network, a real-estate crowdfunding pioneer. **[Membership pricing not found in accessible sources.]**

**What killed it — three simultaneous blows, and the ordering matters:** the parent, Prodigy Network, was already under state and federal lawsuits alleging it was running out of funds and failing to deliver promised returns; **founder Niño died of cancer in May 2020 at 50**; COVID closed the spaces in March 2020. Operations ceased June 2020; the two NoMad buildings sold at a **~20% loss against the $103M paid in 2017**.

**Single most cited factor:** it was never a standalone business — it was an amenity attached to a distressed real-estate financing vehicle, plus total dependence on one charismatic founder.

**Transferable lesson:** Never fund a residency off the balance sheet of a separate, leveraged venture, and make sure the thing survives the founder being unavailable for three months. Name a second person the cohort trusts.

## 7. Soho House (London, 1995) — the best first-cohort mechanic in this entire study

**Recruitment:** Nick Jones already ran Café Bohème at street level. The club went upstairs at **40 Greek Street**. The first members were **the restaurant's existing regulars** — and, crucially, those regulars **formed the first membership committee themselves.** In **June 1994**, seven months before opening, Jones ran a **hard-hat tour of the building site with cocktails served out of cement mixers**. It opened **13 January 1995 with 500 members already signed.**

**Timeline:** ~7 months from first ritual gathering to opening, on top of an existing venue and existing audience.

**Money:** seeded from restaurant cash flow plus modest bank loans — no venture capital, which wasn't available for "creative clubs." First rural extension, Babington House, 1998. **[1995 membership fee not found.]** Note the long-run counterpoint: Soho House has **run at a net loss for nearly three decades** and went private in 2025 at $2.7B. The *first house* worked; the scaling did not.

**Single most cited success factor:** the club was built on top of an audience that already physically gathered somewhere, and membership curation was handed to members from day one.

**Transferable lesson — probably the highest-value one here:** run the **cement-mixer event**. Bring 15–25 candidate residents to the unfinished Italian site in 2026, before anything is ready, and let that group nominate the 2027 cohort. Selection by peers, not by you.

## 8. Kinfolk & Monocle — media first, place second

**Monocle:** launched February 2007 by Tyler Brûlé, backed by private investors, initial print run 150,000, ten issues a year. It then layered on Monocle Radio (2011), shops and cafés — physical spaces arriving **4+ years after** the audience existed, each one small and each one an extension of the brand rather than a bet.

**Kinfolk:** founded July 2011 by two couples (Nathan Williams & Katie Searle-Williams, Doug & Paige Bischoff). Growth came from **hundreds of community-hosted events per year in cities worldwide** — dinners and workshops run by readers, not by the company. Near-zero marginal cost, and it produced local nodes organically. The later Ouur expansion into apparel and home goods was the overreach.

**Single most cited success factor:** the gathering format was **franchised to the audience for free**. Readers hosted; the brand supplied only the aesthetic template.

**Transferable lesson:** Publish before you host. If you can't get 300–500 engaged newsletter subscribers for the Italy project in 2026, the 2027 cohort of 20 will be a struggle to fill and — worse — you'll have no way to select. And consider letting readers run small self-organised dinners in their own cities as the qualifying filter.

## 9. Modern co-living failures — WeLive, Common, Roam

| | What happened | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| **WeLive** | WeWork's residential arm. Projected **34,000 residents across 10.4M sq ft at 91% occupancy by 2018**. Built **two buildings**. Killed 2020. | Two locations, both loss-making |
| **Common** | Largest co-living operator in North America. **Chapter 7 liquidation, June 2024.** Never profitable. Crushed by overhead and rising interest rates; also drowned in tenant complaints (roommate conflict, sanitation, security) that forced it to double support headcount. | — |
| **Roam** | Premium co-living for nomads (Bali, Miami, Tokyo, London). Leased buildings in expensive cities at the top of a cycle, ran them at a loss on speculative capital. | ~$500/week |

**The underlying arithmetic (Pieter Levels).** The target customer has about **$1,000/month, or $30/day**, to spend on rent. Co-living's cost structure forces **$100–200/night = $3,000–6,000/month**. In the cheap destinations where remote workers actually go, hotels and Airbnbs cost 2–4x a long-term rental, so co-living has to beat **$30/night** alternatives offering the same thing. It can't. Result: either heavy VC subsidy or hotel pricing to a market that came for affordability.

**Base rates:** **over 60% of co-living operators fail within 24 months.** Best-case asset-light margins are **20–25%**. And the sharpest operational insight in the literature: **"high occupancy is a lagging indicator of past effort, not a guarantee of future success"** — early full houses achieved through founder hustle and discounting mask economics that break the moment the founder stops hustling.

**Transferable lesson:** A 20-person, time-boxed, pre-paid residency is a fundamentally *better* business than co-living precisely because it has no vacancy risk, no lease liability between cohorts, and cash collected in advance. Protect that. The moment you sign a 12-month lease to "keep the place alive between cohorts," you have converted into the business model that kills 60% of operators in two years.

## 10. Italian borgo revival — albergo diffuso vs. 1€ houses

**Albergo diffuso (the model that worked).** Born in **Carnia, Friuli, 1982**, out of reconstruction after the 1976 earthquake; formalised by tourism professor **Giancarlo Dall'Ara**. Pilot: **Borgo Maranzanis, Comeglians**. First purpose-designed implementation: **San Leo, 1989 — seven years after the pilot.** Today roughly **140 in Italy**, ~40 formally recognised by the national association, with **13 regions having passed enabling legislation.**

The rules are strict and they are the reason it works:
- **No new construction** — only recovery of existing vacant houses
- A **reception/restaurant building in the town centre**, to force guests through the community
- Guest rooms within **~200 m (650 ft)** of that reception
- **A single management entity** for the whole scattered property

**~90% stay open 12 months a year.** De-seasonalisation is the economic engine — it's what makes a scattered, small-inventory operation viable at all.

**Why individual ones fail:** over-commercialisation diluting authenticity → falling occupancy; gentrification displacing the residents who *are* the product; and weak local ownership. The best-documented success (Sexten) is run by **local cooperatives** that manage both renovation and operations.

**1€ houses (the model that mostly didn't).**
- **Sambuca di Sicilia — the success case.** CNN and NYT coverage created a demand shock; the town converted to **sealed-bid auctions starting at €1**. Dozens of foreign buyers, a real expat community. Prices then rose with the publicity, so genuine €1 deals became rare.
- **Ollolai — the underperformer.** Since 2018, **only 10 homes actually sold for €1 and renovated.** In 2024 there were **22 renovation-grant requests and insufficient funds to cover even half.**
- **Mechanism:** buyers post a completion guarantee (e.g. **€5,000 at Cattolica Eraclea**), refunded only on finishing the renovation on schedule.
- **Reality gap:** buyers expect 6–12 months; actual timelines run **years**, with 6+ months for permits, scarce contractors, and seasonal access.

**Single most cited difference:** Sambuca paired the giveaway with a **media moment and an administrative concierge layer**; Ollolai gave away houses and left buyers to fend for themselves. Free property is not the bottleneck — **renovation capital, permits and contractors are.**

**Transferable lesson:** Do not acquire property in 2027. Partner with an existing **albergo diffuso** — they are purpose-built for exactly this: 20 people distributed across restored village houses, one management entity, a central reception forcing daily collision, and they *want* shoulder-season occupancy. This converts your single largest capex and regulatory risk into a negotiated per-night rate, and it hands you local legitimacy on day one.

# Synthesis

## The 5 recurring patterns of projects that made it past cohort one

**1. The first cohort came from an audience that already existed and already gathered.** Soho House drew on Café Bohème's regulars. Zuzalu drew on four named research communities. Edge Esmeralda drew on prior Edge City attendees and Devon Zuegel's followers. Cabin drew on seven friends. **Nobody in this study successfully recruited strangers for cohort one.** Every project that tried to (Culdesac, Common, WeLive) had to buy them with discounts.

**2. Invitation rights were delegated to trusted humans, not to a form.** Zuzalu's ~12 inviters. Soho House's member-run committee formed *before opening*. Edge City's skip-the-application codes issued to allied newsletters. The pattern is a **spoke model**: a handful of curators each responsible for a few seats, which produces both quality and diversity without the founder personally vetting anyone.

**3. Duration long enough to change behaviour, short enough to be reversible.** Two months at Zuzalu and Vitalia; one month at Edge Esmeralda. Edge City's own formulation is the sharpest: *"Every popup ends, and that is the most important part of the design"* — the end date is what gives people permission to behave differently, and what lets people with jobs, kids and mortgages participate at all.

**4. Deliberate, priced-in integration with the host town.** Edge Esmeralda's ~$200 local ticket against ~$2,000 for outsiders. Albergo diffuso's mandatory town-centre reception. Sambuca's municipal partnership. The counter-examples — Vitalia's condescension toward neighbouring Honduran villages, Ollolai's absent support layer — are exactly where the friction shows up.

**5. Programming supplied by residents, not organisers.** 93% of Edge Esmeralda's sessions were community-organised. Zuzalu's cold plunges, karaoke and sub-events were all bottom-up. Kinfolk franchised its entire event strategy to readers. This is simultaneously the biggest cost saving and the strongest signal of genuine belonging.

## The 5 recurring causes of death

**1. Fixed real-estate cost carried between cohorts.** Roam leased expensive buildings and ran them at a loss. WeLive's buildout costs against low occupancy. Common's overhead against rising rates. The Assemblage's $103M buildings sold at a 20% loss. **Every single project in this study that owned or long-leased property lost money; every one that rented per-event did better.**

**2. The price the target customer can pay is below the price the model requires.** Levels' arithmetic: customers have ~$1,000/month, the cost structure demands $3,000–6,000/month. Roam died precisely in that gap — premium pricing into a market that came for affordability. Any project that closes this gap with subsidy is running a clock, not a business.

**3. Scaling before cohort two was profitable.** WeLive projected 34,000 residents and built two buildings. Common expanded aggressively into liquidation. Kinfolk's Ouur overreach. Soho House has lost money for ~30 years *because of* expansion, even though the first house worked. The failure is almost never the first site.

**4. Coordination overhead eating the thing it was meant to coordinate.** Cabin's explicit wind-down reason: operational overhead exceeded what the network could sustain. Zuzalu's undefined governance and opaque selection were its most-cited criticism. Governance machinery is a tax that small communities cannot pay.

**5. Single-point dependency — one founder, one parent company, or one host regime.** The Assemblage lost its founder and its parent's solvency in the same quarter. Vitalia's entire legal basis is a politically contested SEZ. Ollolai's buyers depended on grant funds that covered under half of even 22 requests.

## The composite play for 20 paid residents in Italy, 2027

Synthesising the above into the specific decisions this implies:

- **2026 Q3–Q4:** publish first. Aim for 300–500 engaged subscribers. Kinfolk's test — can readers be persuaded to host their own small dinners? — is your qualification filter and costs nothing.
- **2026 Q4:** run the **cement-mixer event**. 15–25 candidates at the unfinished Italian site. That group becomes your membership committee and nominates the 2027 twenty.
- **Selection:** 5–8 named inviters, ~3 seats each, drawn from **deliberately different disciplines** (Zuzalu's four-tribe structure at 1/10th scale). Application form after the invite, not before.
- **Site:** partner with an existing **albergo diffuso** in shoulder season. Zero capex, single management counterparty, town-centre reception built into the model, and they need your off-season nights.
- **Duration:** 4–6 weeks with a hard end date. Everyone arrives the same day — Edge City's late-arrival integration problem is real at 1,300 people and worse at 20.
- **Pricing:** unbundle like Vitalia. A programme fee that covers your fixed costs standing alone, plus accommodation passed through at negotiated rates. Add a token-priced local tier (Edge City's ~10:1 ratio) as licence-to-operate.
- **Governance:** none. One entity, one decision-maker, no token, for the first three cohorts.
- **Success metric:** not headcount. **Return rate and stay-on rate** — Vitalia's 40–50 stayers out of 500 visitors is the number that mattered, not the 500.
- **The trap to avoid:** signing any lease to keep the place warm between cohorts. That single decision converts a de-risked, pre-paid, zero-vacancy business into the co-living model that kills 60% of operators inside 24 months.
