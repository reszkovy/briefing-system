Picatrix

TL;DR

The Ghayat al-Hakim ("The Goal of the Sage"), Latinized as Picatrix: a 10th-century compendium of astral magic written in al-Andalus, translated at the court of Alfonso X, and the single most influential magic manual in Western history.

On this page
  1. Who wrote it
  2. What’s inside
  3. The road to the Renaissance
  4. How to read it

The Picatrix is what happened to the Hermetic current after antiquity: not a dialogue on mind and rebirth like the Corpus Hermeticum, but a working compendium — the most influential manual of astral magic ever written. Its Arabic original, the Ghayat al-Hakim (“The Goal of the Sage”), was composed in al-Andalus; its author states he finished it around 348 AH (c. 959 CE), after completing its companion volume, the Rutbat al-Hakim.

Who wrote it

For centuries the book was misattributed to the mathematician Maslama al-Majriti. In 1996 Maribel Fierro identified the real author as Maslama al-Qurtubi (d. 964), a scholar from Córdoba — an identification confirmed and extended by Godefroid de Callataÿ, which also settled the dating: 10th century, not the mid-11th as older scholarship held. Even the Latin name remains a puzzle: whether “Picatrix” is a corruption of “Buqratis” (Hippocrates) or a nickname of the author is unresolved.

What’s inside

Talismans, planetary invocations, elections of astrologically favourable moments, suffumigations — but embedded in a theory: rays, spiritus, a cosmic hierarchy through which celestial influence descends into matter. The book cites Hermes and Sabian rites extensively, synthesizing Hermetic, Sabian and Neoplatonic material. It is a compilation rather than a doctrinal Hermetic treatise — the practical cousin of the statue-animating passages in the Asclepius, and of the technical tradition that also produced the Emerald Tablet.

The road to the Renaissance

Between 1256 and 1258, Alfonso X of Castile ordered the Arabic translated into Castilian; a Latin version followed in the 13th century, and with it the name Picatrix. Through that Latin text the book reached the Renaissance: Ficino’s and Agrippa’s astral magic stand demonstrably downstream of it. The route runs through the translation workshops of Iberia — Córdoba to Toledo — mapped in our Atlas, a later chapter of the story that began in Alexandria.

How to read it

The scholarly landmarks are David Pingree’s edition of the Latin version (Warburg Institute, 1986) and the Attrell & Porreca English translation of that Latin (Penn State UP, 2019) — the standard way into the text in English; work on translating directly from the Arabic continues. Both are modern and in copyright; there is no public-domain English Picatrix, so treat free PDFs online accordingly. Modern occult publishing markets the book as a plug-and-play grimoire; historically it is a learned philosophical-magical compendium in which the theory matters as much as the recipes.

Fair questions

Is the Picatrix dangerous?

It is a 10th-century historical document, and we treat it as one. Its 'danger' in medieval and early modern eyes was real — astral magic sat at the edge of licit knowledge — but for a modern reader the risks are prosaic: bad translations, credulous framing, and occult marketing that sells a learned compendium as a spellbook. Read it with a historian's questions, not a shopping list.

Why is it called Picatrix?

Nobody is sure. The name appears with the Latin translation; proposals include a corruption of 'Buqratis' (the Arabic form of Hippocrates) or a nickname of the author. The question is genuinely unresolved.

Is the Picatrix a Hermetic text?

It cites Hermes and Sabian rites extensively and carries Hermetic material, but it is a compilation of astral magic, not a doctrinal treatise like the Corpus Hermeticum. It belongs to the technical, practical side of the tradition.
