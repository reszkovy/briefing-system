The Stobaean Fragments

TL;DR

Nearly thirty Hermetic excerpts preserved only because a 5th-century compiler, Johannes Stobaeus, copied them into an anthology for his son. They include the Kore Kosmou, where Isis — not Hermes — does the teaching, and they reached complete scholarly English only in 2018.

On this page
  1. What’s inside
  2. Virgin or pupil?
  3. How to read it

Around the early 5th century CE, a Macedonian named Johannes Stobaeus assembled a vast anthology of Greek literature for his son’s education. Buried in it are nearly thirty Hermetic excerpts (numbered SH 1–29) that appear nowhere in the Corpus Hermeticum — for most of them, Stobaeus is the sole surviving source. One father’s reading list is the reason a third of the philosophical Hermetica exists at all.

What’s inside

Dialogues of Hermes with Tat and with Ammon — and, unexpectedly, a group in which the teacher is Isis, instructing her son Horus. The centrepiece is the Korē Kosmou (SH 23, with the related SH 24–27): a mythological cosmogony of souls created, rebelling, and punished by embodiment; of civilizing knowledge descending; of Egypt as the sacred centre. It is the most explicitly Egyptian text in the philosophical Hermetica, usually dated to the 2nd–3rd century CE — the same milieu as the Asclepius, on the far side of the tradition from the visionary ascent of nous in Poimandres.

Virgin or pupil?

Even the famous title is a live question. Greek korē means both “virgin/maiden” and “pupil of the eye,” so Korē Kosmou can be read as “Virgin of the World” or “Pupil of the Eye of the World” — and scholars genuinely disagree. Popular editions settled on “The Virgin of the World” after Kingsford and Maitland’s Theosophical translation of 1885 and market it as Isis’ “lost gospel,” usually without mentioning the double meaning or the fact that everything we have is excerpts, cut to a 5th-century compiler’s taste. Related open questions: whether SH 23–27 come from one large lost work or several, and how the Isis–Horus line relates to the Hermes–Tat transmission.

How to read it

For a century, English readers had only Walter Scott’s 1924 version — notorious for rewriting the Greek he judged corrupt — or Mead’s Theosophically framed 1906 rendering. The first complete modern scholarly English translation is M. David Litwa, Hermetica II (Cambridge, 2018), which covers all the Stobaean excerpts plus papyrus fragments and ancient testimonies, completing Copenhaver’s 1992 volume. Litwa is in copyright — buy it or borrow it; the free versions online are Scott and Mead, usable only if you know what they are. Where the tradition’s geography ran — Alexandria and beyond — is mapped in step 03 and the Atlas.

Fair questions

What does Kore Kosmou actually mean?

Both 'Virgin of the World' and 'Pupil (of the eye) of the World' are defensible — Greek korē carries both senses, and scholars still disagree. Popular editions print 'Virgin of the World' after an 1885 Theosophical translation, without flagging the ambiguity.

Why aren't these fragments in the Corpus Hermeticum?

The Corpus is a separate Byzantine compilation; Stobaeus excerpted from Hermetic writings that never entered it. His anthology is the sole source for most of these texts — two independent survival channels for one tradition.

Where can I read them in English?

The complete scholarly translation is Litwa's Hermetica II (2018), in copyright. The free options — Scott (1924) and Mead (1906) — predate the critical text; Scott heavily rewrote passages. Read them as historical artifacts, not as the standard.
