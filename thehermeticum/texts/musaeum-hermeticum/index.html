Musaeum Hermeticum

TL;DR

A Latin anthology of alchemical treatises published in Frankfurt in 1625 and greatly expanded in 1678 to twenty-one tracts, illustrated by the Merian workshop. Despite the name, it contains no ancient Hermetica — here 'hermetic' means alchemy.

On this page
  1. What’s inside
  2. One Victorian filter
  3. How to read it

Start with the trap in the title: the Musaeum Hermeticum — the “Hermetic museum” — contains not one line of the ancient Hermetica. No Corpus Hermeticum, no Asclepius, not even the Emerald Tablet. By the 17th century, “hermetic” had come to mean the ars hermetica — alchemy, the craft under the patronage of Hermes Trismegistus (the same shift that gave us “hermetically sealed”). This book is the bridge between the ancient corpus and early-modern laboratory alchemy — a different tradition wearing the same name.

What’s inside

The first edition — Frankfurt, 1625, published by Lucas Jennis, in Latin (Wikipedia says German; that is an error, arising from the German sibling volume Jennis issued the same year, the Dyas chymica tripartita). The canonical edition is the revised and enlarged 1678 Frankfurt printing: twenty-one treatises by its own title page, adding Sendivogius, Helvetius’ Golden Calf — a 1666 eyewitness account of a transmutation in The Hague — and the works of “Eirenaeus Philalethes,” long imagined a mysterious adept and identified by William Newman as George Starkey (1628–1665), the Harvard-trained chymist. The volume’s visual fame rests on its engravings from the Merian workshop, above all the fifteen emblem plates of the Book of Lambspring — images that still circulate online as “ancient alchemical symbols,” usually without book, engraver or date attached.

One Victorian filter

Everything English readers know as “the Hermetic Museum” passes through a single edition: A. E. Waite’s The Hermetic Museum, Restored and Enlarged (London, 1893, 250 copies). Waite did not translate it himself — an unidentified translator prepared the base version, which Waite revised — it condenses the Latin’s “prolixity” by its own admission, and Waite’s introductions read the tracts as veiled spiritual mysticism. Modern historians (Newman, Principe) have shown that much of this literature encodes reproducible laboratory practice — though several tracts are genuinely soaked in devotional language; the honest reading is that the anthology mixes both. No complete modern scholarly English translation exists.

How to read it

Legally and free: the 1678 Latin is scanned in high resolution at e-rara.ch (ETH Zürich) and on the Internet Archive (Getty copy); Waite’s 1893 English is public domain and available on the Internet Archive and Sacred Texts. The engravings (Merian d. 1650) are public domain too. Read Waite for access, with his limitations in mind — and remember which century, and which “hermetic,” you are in. The larger story of alchemy’s place in the tradition begins back in Alexandria.

Fair questions

Is the Musaeum Hermeticum part of the Corpus Hermeticum?

No. Despite the name it contains no ancient Hermetic texts at all — it is a 17th-century anthology of alchemical treatises. By then 'hermetic' meant the art of alchemy, as in 'hermetically sealed'.

Was it first published in German?

No — the 1625 first edition is in Latin, with nine treatises. The confusion (repeated by Wikipedia) comes from a German anthology the same publisher issued the same year, the Dyas chymica tripartita, which shares texts and plates.

Where can I read it legally for free?

The 1678 Latin edition is scanned at e-rara.ch (ETH Zürich) and on the Internet Archive (Getty Research Institute copy); Waite's 1893 English translation is public domain on the Internet Archive and Sacred Texts. The Merian-workshop engravings are public domain as well.
