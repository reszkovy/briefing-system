Zosimos of Panopolis

TL;DR

Zosimos of Panopolis (fl. c. 300 CE, Upper Egypt) is the first alchemist in history known by name as a real, datable author. His writings combine working laboratory chemistry with visionary allegory — and he is one of the earliest witnesses that the Corpus Hermeticum was actually read and lived, not just written.

The first chemist we know by name had nightmares about the work. In his famous dream-lessons — the “Visions” — a priest named Ion declares he has been dismembered by the sword, burned and transformed “from body into spirit,” while men boil in bubbling water around a bowl-shaped altar. Zosimos wakes, and interprets: the torments are the operations. The suffering of matter is its refinement — and, read technically, the sequence maps onto distillation and the colouring of metals.

A real person, a real date

Unlike the legendary names before him — Pseudo-Democritus, Maria the Jewess, Agathodaimon, whom he himself preserves by quotation — Zosimos is datable: he cites Julius Africanus and mentions the Serapeum of Alexandria as still standing, which fixes him around 300 CE. He worked in Panopolis (modern Akhmim) in Upper Egypt and addressed nearly everything to a woman, Theosebeia — student, patron, or literary device, and either way one of the named women at the very origin of chemistry. His equipment was real: multi-piped stills, the kērotakis reflux apparatus, furnaces, “divine water.”

The hinge of the Hermetic tradition

Zosimos matters to this site because he is the living link between the technical and philosophical Hermetica. In the Final Count he tells Theosebeia to “hasten towards Poimenandres” and receive “baptism in the krater” — naming treatises of the Corpus Hermeticum (CH I and CH IV) as a devotional path. That one sentence is among the best evidence that around 300 CE the Hermetica were lived, not just copied. In On the Letter Omega he cites Hermes on the sage who stands above fate — alchemy and the philosophy of nous as one curriculum, in one person.

What he was not

He did not invent alchemy — he is the first named author in an existing tradition. The Visions record no initiation ritual; they are carefully composed allegory. And he was no gold-making charlatan: he attacks fraudulent tincture-mongers himself. The modern fame of Zosimos owes much to C. G. Jung, who from 1937 read the Visions as archetypal dream material — a 20th-century reception worth knowing about, not an account of what a Greco-Egyptian alchemist meant.

Fair questions

Was Zosimos the first alchemist?

He is the first alchemist known by name as a real, datable individual. Alchemy already existed — Pseudo-Democritus and Maria the Jewess precede him — but they survive mainly through his quotations.

What are the Visions of Zosimos?

Three dream-lessons in his Authentic Memoirs: a priest is dismembered and transformed at a bowl-shaped altar. Most scholars read them as deliberate allegory of laboratory operations, made famous in the 20th century by Jung's psychological reading.

Was Zosimos a Hermetist, a Gnostic, or a Christian?

The labels blur: he quotes the Hermetica, Enochic myth, and figures revered in Gnostic circles in one breath. He shows that around 300 CE Egyptian esoteric religion was a shared reservoir the modern categories postdate.
