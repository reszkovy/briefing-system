Pico della
Mirandola

TL;DR

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494) published 900 theses drawn from every tradition he could read and invited all of Europe to debate them; the pope condemned some as heretical. His famous Oration on the Dignity of Man — which opens by quoting the Hermetic Asclepius — was never delivered, never published in his lifetime, and is about ascending to angelic existence, not celebrating secular man.

On this page

  2. The myth of the manifesto
  3. How Hermetic was Pico?

At the end of 1486, a 23-year-old count published nearly 900 propositions drawn from Christian, Muslim, Jewish and pagan authorities and invited every scholar in Europe to Rome to debate them. Pope Innocent VIII answered instead: thirteen theses indicted, seven condemned as heretical, then the whole collection denounced. Pico briefly fled Italy. The debate never happened — and neither did the speech written to open it.

A great miracle is man

That speech is the famous Oration — and it opens with a Hermetic sentence. Pico’s first authority is the Asclepius: magnum miraculum est homo, “a great miracle is man.” In the Hermetic original, man is a miracle because he is double — mortal in body, divine in mind, placed to tend the earth and contemplate heaven. Pico builds on that: a being with no fixed nature, free to sink to the beast or rise toward the angel. The theme is the soul’s transformative ascent through philosophy, magic and Kabbalah — Pico was the first Christian thinker to take Jewish Kabbalah seriously as evidence for Christian doctrine.

The myth of the manifesto

Textbooks call the Oration “the manifesto of the Renaissance.” The facts are stranger: it was never delivered, never published by Pico, and the title “On the Dignity of Man” was attached after his death — officially only in 1557. Specialist consensus (Copenhaver’s Magic and the Dignity of Man, 2019) now reads it as a mystical-Kabbalistic ascent text, not a proto-modern celebration of secular humanity. The most quoted Renaissance speech is a document almost nobody in the Renaissance heard.

How Hermetic was Pico?

Honest answer: less than the opening quote suggests. Per the Stanford Encyclopedia, Pico preferred Orphic and Chaldean sources and downplayed the Hermetica relative to Ficino. He belongs to the great occult-philosophy synthesis rather than to Hermeticism strictly speaking — which makes his choice of epigraph the more telling: even for a thinker who ranked other sources higher, Hermes was the voice you opened with. He died in Florence on 17 November 1494, aged thirty-one. Where he sits in the wider story: the Atlas.

Fair questions

What is Pico della Mirandola famous for?

The 900 Theses of 1486 — an invitation to debate propositions from every tradition, partly condemned by the pope — and the Oration on the Dignity of Man written to introduce them.

Did Pico ever deliver the Oration on the Dignity of Man?

No. The debate was suppressed, the speech was never delivered, Pico never published it, and its famous title was attached after his death — officially only in 1557.

Was Pico a Hermeticist?

Only loosely. He opens the Oration quoting the Hermetic Asclepius, but he preferred Orphic, Chaldean and Kabbalistic sources and downplayed the Hermetica compared with Ficino.
