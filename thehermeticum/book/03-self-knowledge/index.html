Self-Knowledge

You will not manage a system you do not observe — and the worst-observed system in your life is you.

Opening signal

There is a kind of failure that does not come from not knowing. You know you should not take this project. You know how conversations held at eleven at night end. You know the sign, in yourself, of the moment when you stop deciding and start defending. And you do it again anyway, usually in full awareness while it is happening, which is the most humiliating version of the experience.

We usually call this a lack of character. It is a mistaken diagnosis, and a costly one, because it leads to the single remedy that is certain not to work: a resolution. The real reason is more technical. Between your knowledge of yourself and your behaviour stands something you cannot see at the moment of acting — a state, a trigger, an old agreement with yourself made under different circumstances. The knowledge is in one room, the behaviour in another, and the door between them closes exactly when things get hot.

There is also a second version of the same problem, quieter and commoner among people who think about themselves a good deal: self-knowledge as a hobby. You understand your mechanisms, you can describe them, you have a vocabulary for them — and that understanding does not translate into one single changed decision. An insight that does not end in a movement stops being an insight. It becomes running commentary on your own life.

This chapter is about the difference between those two things: between thinking about yourself and observing yourself.

Source

The hermetic tradition. The sharpest text is Corpus Hermeticum XIII — the treatise on rebirth, in which Hermes leads his pupil Tat through palingenesis, the rebirth accomplished within this life. What is striking in it is the arithmetic. Ten divine powers — knowledge of God, joy, self-control, endurance, justice, generosity, truth, the Good, life, light — descend into the initiate and drive out twelve “torments”: ignorance, grief, incontinence, desire, injustice, greed, deceit, envy, treachery, anger, rashness, malice. The twelve are explicitly zodiacal (CH XIII.12): the decad of powers displaces the dodecad of the stars. The text therefore takes astral determinism seriously enough to describe the escape from it in the categories of astrology itself, sign by sign. It insists at the same time that this knowledge cannot be transmitted by argument (CH XIII.2–3) — it is experienced, not proved. Garth Fowden summarised the “way of Hermes” as leading from knowledge of the world, through knowledge of the self, to knowledge of God; knowledge of the self is the middle step there, never the goal.

What is not in it. CH XIII is not psychology, does not describe work on oneself in our sense, and was not written so that anyone could make an exercise of it. The list of twelve torments is a catalogue of vices within a particular cosmology, not a diagnostics of behaviour.

Contemporary sources. Richard Nisbett and Timothy Wilson published in 1977 a paper, Telling More Than We Can Know, in which they showed that people regularly give reasons for their choices that could not have been the real reasons — and give them with complete conviction. Tasha Eurich’s research on self-awareness distinguishes an internal version of it (understanding oneself) from an external one (knowing how others see us), and indicates that the correlation between the two can be negligible; introspection alone does not guarantee accuracy.

Limits. The research on confabulation was conducted under laboratory conditions and on narrow tasks. Estimates of how many people are genuinely self-aware rest on self-report and questionnaires — that is, on the very instrument whose reliability they themselves call into question.

Our reading

What follows is our position, not the content of CH XIII and not a conclusion from the research cited. From the sources we take three things and move them deliberately outside their original context.

You will not manage a system you do not observe. And knowing yourself is neither a mood nor a talent for introspection — it is a discipline of seeing, which is to say a repeatable activity, with a rule, a record and a review. If the research says anything unambiguous, it is precisely that thinking about yourself without a record is unreliable.

The crucial shift fits into one question. Do not ask only: who am I? — that is a question about identity, so the answer will always be a self-portrait, and self-portraits are kind. Ask also: what system is running me today? Who set the order of my affairs, whose urgency became mine, what role am I defending in this conversation, whose rhythm have I been living in for the last three weeks. These are questions about mechanics, so they have testable answers.

From CH XIII we take three operations.

  • The torments have names, and there is a countable number of them. Not “something is blocking me”, but: envy. Not “I am going through a difficult period”, but: greed and rashness, in that order, in those situations. Naming is the first move of the operation, not its ornament.
  • Displacement works by introduction, not by struggle. In the text the torments are not defeated — they are displaced by powers that take their place. The difference is practical: a habit can rarely be removed by a vacuum, but it can be displaced by another habit that occupies the same hour of the day, the same state and the same need.
  • Knowledge of yourself cannot be had second-hand. From somebody else’s description you get a vocabulary at most; the mechanism is learned on yourself and in real time.

To this we add two things that are not in the source. First: observation has to be separated from judgement, and not for therapeutic reasons but for epistemic ones. An observer who is also a prosecutor gets testimony prepared for the interrogation. A man who pays with shame for every note he makes about himself begins, in the second week, to write down a prettier version — and loses the only data he came for. Second: observation exists in order to change the structure, not in order to understand better. Insight is raw material, not product. If after a month of careful self-observation you have not changed one single thing about how you arrange the day, whom you say yes to, and where you draw the line — you were not observing, you were reading about yourself.

And the hardest thing: self-knowledge has a ceiling. There are things about yourself you will not see on your own, because precisely the part that is looking has an interest in the result. For those things another person is needed — one who has the right to tell you something uncomfortable and is not counting on your gratitude. Whoever has not a single such person in his life is observing from one point, and will have a constant, invisible hole in what he sees.

Operational translation

Work. Your postponing is information, not a fault. The things you systematically postpone usually fall into three categories: those you do not know how to do (missing competence), those where you fear being judged (status risk), and those that should not be yours at all (lack of alignment). Each requires a different move, and all three look identical from outside. Before you add discipline, check which of the three is in front of you.

Relationships. It is worth knowing who holds the remote control to your state. This is not about cutting people off — it is about being aware that there are people after a conversation with whom you are not yourself for three hours, and that planning important decisions for those three hours is simply a technical error. A separate and harder observation: in which relationships are you playing a role that was once necessary and now only tires you.

Decisions. The surest indicator of a bad decision is not its content but the state it was taken from. Decisions made out of desperation, out of haste, or out of a wish to end discomfort are bad systematically, however well they sound at the moment of being made. Hence a simple operational rule: never out of desperation. If you have to do it in order for the pain to stop, that is not a choice, it is a symptom.

Money. Spending is a more honest record of values than declarations, and it is uncomfortable in exactly the degree to which it is useful. Three months of bank statements will tell you more about your priorities than three hours of thinking about them.

Technology. It is worth knowing your own gesture of escape — that one movement of the hand, always made at the same moment: at a difficult sentence, at uncertainty, at boredom, at an approaching emotion. That gesture is the cleanest measurement available of your threshold of discomfort. You do not have to eliminate it straight away. It is enough to start noticing it while it happens, because then it stops being automatic.

Embodied check

Most of what we take for insights about ourselves are reports about state. “This project is pointless”, after a sleepless night, is not an assessment of the project. “This relationship is exhausting me”, in the third week of overload, is not an assessment of the relationship. The rule is simple and saves a good many decisions: do not draw conclusions about your life in a state in which you would not draw conclusions about your work.

It works the other way round too, and that is the more useful direction. The body recognises a trigger before the head does. Before you have managed to name what is happening in this conversation, your pulse is already up, your breath shallow, your shoulders drawn in — or, more often than we admit, there is a sudden, unaccountable need to reach for the phone. These are the first data, available a dozen or so seconds before the interpretation. Whoever learns to notice them while they happen gets those seconds at his disposal, and a choice fits inside them.

That is why one of the columns in this chapter’s register concerns nothing but the state before the event: the hour, sleep, food, what was happening in the previous hour. It is the column that most often solves the puzzle and the one most often left out, because it seems beside the point. It is not beside the point. A trigger rarely works alone — it works when it meets the right state, and the state is the part you can actually affect.

Recognising your state before deciding has here the simplest possible version: before you answer something that has raised your pulse, notice that your pulse has risen. Nothing more. That is enough for the reaction to stop being automatic.

We are not dealing with physiology here and we propose no breathing technique. This is simply the place where information about yourself is obtained most cheaply.

Practice

A trigger log — a seven-day experiment

A register of triggers. Not a diary of feelings and not a confession — a diagnostic instrument whose aim is to find repetition. A single outburst tells you nothing. Three outbursts at the same point of the day, after the same type of conversation, are already a mechanism — and a mechanism can be rebuilt.

Preparation. One page, five columns. You write only when something has gone off: irritation, withdrawal, a sudden need to escape into the phone, a yes said against yourself, the defence of a position you did not even want to defend.

  • Column 1 — the situation. Facts, one sentence. Who, where, what was said. No interpretation.
  • Column 2 — the state before. The hour, sleep, food, what you were doing in the previous hour. It is the column that most often solves the puzzle and the one most often left out.
  • Column 3 — the reaction. What you did, literally. Not what you felt — what your body, your mouth and your hands did.
  • Column 4 — what I was defending. One word: competence, control, status, calm, image, time, closeness. This is the hardest column and for the first few days it will be empty or false. That is normal.
  • Column 5 — the cost. What it cost: minutes, a relationship, a decision, an evening.

Day 4 — a first look, ten minutes. Do not look for meaning. Look for repetitions. Circle everything that has appeared at least twice: the same hours, the same people, the same type of situation, the same word in column four.

Day 7 — the review proper, forty minutes. Four questions in writing. Which trigger comes back most often? What condition precedes it in the second column almost every time? What am I defending in these situations, and does it still require defending? What exactly did this week cost me — not in general, but in numbers and names?

One structural move. At the end choose one change, and a change of condition, not a resolution to be better. A condition is testable and requires no willpower. Examples from actual practice: no difficult conversations after nine at night; agreement to a project only after a night’s sleep; before answering a message that raises the pulse, one lap around the table; I never count money on a Sunday evening. Dull. Effective.

Where this comes from. The form is ours and pretends to be nothing more. The structure beneath it — name it, count it, displace it by introducing something that takes the same place — is our reading of the operation in CH XIII, deliberately carried outside its original context.

What it does not promise. This is not therapy and does not replace it; if what comes out of the register is heavier than a habit — trauma, addiction, depression — the right instrument is a qualified person, not a table. Seven days is also not enough for seasonal patterns, or for those that surface once a quarter; if the week has brought no repetition at all, that is information and not a failure — repeat the register in another month. And one last thing: the register itself changes nothing. What changes things is the one condition you introduce after it.

Limits

Observation can be an escape from change. The commonest failure of this chapter is not that someone refuses to look at himself, but that he looks indefinitely. Understanding your own mechanism yields enough satisfaction to stand in for the move. That is why the register runs seven days, with one review and one change — and not as an open-ended process of getting to know yourself.

Reflection turns into rumination. The line is clear: reflection looks for mechanics and ends in a conclusion; rumination circles the same scene and ends in feeling worse. If on the third evening you are back at the same conversation with nothing new, this is no longer observation. Close the notebook.

Not everything in you is there to be repaired. Part of what you see in the register is a description of your parameters, not of your mistakes. Needing a day of quiet after two days of intense conversation is not a defect to be removed; it is a quantity around which the rest is planned. People who do not make this distinction spend years treating their own constitution.

Not everything requires action. Not everything that can be changed should be changed.

There are things this instrument has no right to. Grief is not a trigger to be rebuilt. Fear for the health of someone close is not a mechanism to be optimised. Trauma is not an entry in column four. An operational register sees mechanics, not history — and where history begins, the right instrument is a qualified person, or simply time.

Ethical question

Self-knowledge turns outward easily. Whoever observes his own triggers for a week begins, in the second week, to see other people’s — and that is the moment the instrument changes owner. Diagnosing people who did not ask for it is a form of advantage, even when it is said in a gentle voice. “You react like that because…” sounds like concern and works like annulment: the other side loses the right to her own account of the situation. A sentence about somebody else’s mechanism is worth keeping to yourself until someone asks for it.

The vocabulary as an instrument of control. The language of working on yourself — boundaries, needs, triggers, regulation — supplies very effective justifications. It can be used to describe a withdrawal from responsibility, a punishment by silence, or the ending of a relationship as a mature move. That is the difference between agency and power over others: agency changes your conditions, power changes somebody else’s without their consent. The test remains the same as in the previous chapters: would I tell the other person plainly what I am doing and what for — and would she still go along with it.

The cost to me. Sharp self-observation can also be a way of keeping your distance from your own life. A man who knows everything about himself can be a man who lives nothing through without a commentary. It is worth checking now and then whether you are still able to be in a conversation without analysing it at the same time.

Agency increases responsibility. The more you can do, the more carefully you must choose.

Closing formula

A man who does not observe himself is not free of his mechanisms — he is only their unwitting executor, and usually a very efficient one. Observation does not give you control over yourself; that is a promise no honest person will make. It gives something more modest and sufficient: a few seconds of gap between trigger and reaction. Everything we will later call agency fits inside that gap.

Do not ask only who you are — ask what system is running you today.
