Operational Hermeticism · Chapter 08 of 12

Technology

The enemy is not technology — the enemy is capture. A tool that stops carrying out your intention and begins carrying out somebody else’s, with your hands.

Opening signal

You unlock your phone for one specific thing. To check the time of a train. Twenty minutes later you put it down without knowing the time of the train, but with a vague sense that you read something and that something annoyed you. You cannot reconstruct the route you took from one to the other.

We usually explain this to ourselves as a lack of self-discipline. It is a convenient explanation, and a false one. On the other side of the screen, hundreds of very able people and some very good models were at work, with the single task of holding you a few minutes longer. This is not a contest of willpower. It is an asymmetry.

Two camps have formed in response to that asymmetry, and in our view both have already lost. The first is the enthusiasts: everything new is good, lateness in adoption is the only sin, and any question about cost sounds like fear of the future. The second is the catastrophists: switch it off, cut the cord, back to paper, a flip phone, the digital detox as a form of virtue. The first group hands over its agency at a run. The second hands it over in flight. The result is the same: you are not the one deciding.

This chapter takes a third position, considerably less striking. The Modern Hermet is not anti-technology. The Modern Hermet is anti-capture. The question is not whether a tool is good or bad — that is a badly posed question with no answer. The question is one that can be checked: whose intention is this tool carrying out right now?

The question has grown more urgent since the tools began speaking in full sentences. The capture of attention is inconvenient; the capture of judgement is something of another order.

Source

This has to be said honestly at the outset: the hermetic texts do not speak about technology, because they had none in our sense. They do, however, contain one figure that strikes this subject with unsettling precision. The Asclepius has a passage about statues: unable to create a soul, human beings create a dwelling for one — they make images and draw power down into them, so that the object begins to act. For centuries this was read as a description of magic; the text survives mainly in a Latin version from late antiquity, and Coptic fragments from Nag Hammadi confirm its earlier circulation.

The same treatise sits next to the so-called Lament — a prophecy of an Egypt abandoned by its gods, in which the temple remains and the spirit departs from it. The Emerald Tablet adds the opposite movement: what has ascended descends again to the earth and receives the power of things above and things below.

The modern basis is different but convergent. Persuasive design is a documented discipline — B.J. Fogg ran a captology laboratory at Stanford from the nineteen-nineties, and the mechanics of variable reward have been known from research on conditioning since Skinner. On the side of decisions we have well-documented automation bias: Parasuraman and Riley (1997) and Skitka and colleagues (1999) showed that people working with automated prompts make characteristic errors — they miss what the system did not flag, and they carry out what it flagged wrongly, even with access to contradictory data. A separate line of research on cognitive offloading (Risko and Gilbert, 2016) describes how readily we shift memory and calculation onto devices.

This research does not say what to avoid using. It says that trust in a tool grows faster than the tool’s accuracy.

Our reading

This is our position. Technology is not the enemy. Capture is the enemy. Capture has an operational definition: the moment when a tool stops carrying out your intention and begins carrying out somebody else’s — with your hands, your time and your attention, while preserving the full impression that you are still the one deciding.

We read the passage about statues not as a description of magic but as the first description in our culture of an artefact with a built-in intention: a thing into which somebody put a will, and which goes on carrying it out by itself, long after its maker has left. That is exactly what software is. In every system sit somebody’s criteria — locked into a success metric, into a default setting, into what is one click away and what is seven. Hence our fastest test of correspondence: what is in the business model above shows up in your evening below. If you want to know what a tool is for, check what its makers are paid for.

With AI it is the same, only the stakes are higher. AI is not an oracle, an authority or a source of identity. It is an amplifier. It strengthens whatever it is given at the input: a clear intention it turns into leverage, and anxiety and an unmade decision into a very eloquent amplification of confusion.

Which is why the question of this decade is not “what can AI do”, but: what is worth doing with it, and what should stay human? And two things to cut off. It is not about moderation — you can spend ten hours in a tool as its author and twenty minutes in it as its material. It is not about distrust of machines — a model can be a better thinking partner than most of the conversations in a day. It is about direction and nothing else.

The advantage does not belong to whoever generates the most. It belongs to whoever knows what is worth generating.

Operational translation

The concrete part begins where you have to settle what can be delegated. Three layers — and a hard rule that only the first two are passable.

Layer one: execution. Delegate without remorse. Rewriting, tidying, formatting, first drafts, summaries of two hundred pages, searching your own notes, translation, scaffolding code, ten variants to be thrown away. This is low-value work that for decades ate the energy needed elsewhere. Handing it to a machine is not laziness — it is recovering the core.

Layer two: judgement. Delegate the input, never the output. You can ask for arguments against your own thesis, for three framings of a problem, for a note on what you are not seeing. That is an excellent use and it genuinely makes you smarter. But the assessment of which of those framings is true stays on your side. The model does not answer for the consequences. You do. Delegating judgement to an entity that bears no consequences is a category error, not a matter of preference.

Layer three: intention. Here delegation ends. What I want. What is worth it. Who I want to be in five years. Whether this project should exist at all. No model will answer these for you — not because it is too weak, but because the content of the answer is that you arrived at it yourself.

From this division follows the most important observation about the market you will be working in. In the world after AI, intelligence itself gets cheaper. The ability to generate competent text, analysis, code and images stops being a differentiator, because everyone has it. What gets more expensive are the things that cannot be generated: taste — knowing what is good; intention — knowing what for; judgement — knowing when; energy — the capacity to carry a thing to the end; trust — the fact that somebody stakes their own risk on you; and the capacity to choose — the nerve to reject ninety good options for the one right one.

In practice this means one shift in the working week: less time producing, more deciding and closing. It is an unpleasant change, because producing gives the immediate sense that something is happening, while deciding looks from the outside like sitting and staring at a wall.

And two small rules that save a great deal. Do not hand over first contact with a problem — before you ask the model, write three sentences of your own about what you actually think; not to prove your independence to yourself, but so that you have a position of your own, against which the answer will be material rather than revelation. And the second, on a horizon of years: skills you do not practise go quiet faster than we expect. If for a year you write no difficult paragraph yourself, you will not lose the ability to write — you will lose the ability to recognise when a paragraph is good. Delegating execution is free. Delegating practice has a deferred price.

Embodied check

Capture can be detected in the body earlier than in the calendar. A screen leaves a physiological trace, and all you have to do is learn to notice it.

Three readings. Breath. While scrolling and while working through an inbox, many people breathe more shallowly or hold their breath for anything from a few to a dozen seconds. You do not have to believe it — check it on yourself this evening. A held breath is a signal that the nervous system is running in vigilance mode, not in working mode. Posture. The shoulders ride up towards the ears, the jaw clenches, the head pushes forward. After an hour in that position the tiredness is real, even though you have done nothing heavy. The state after putting it down. Ten minutes after the session: is your head clearer and do you want to do something, or are you irritable and tacky? That is the same test of agency as the principle of this chapter, only run at a level that is harder to talk your way out of.

It is worth knowing the two points in the day where the reading matters most. The first hour after waking: if it starts with a screen, the day starts with somebody else’s agenda before you have had time to have one of your own. And the last hour before sleep: light and arousal push back falling asleep, and a short night comes back the next day as worse judgement — which is exactly the resource this chapter is fighting for.

With AI there is a separate difficulty: a conversation with a model gives no bodily feedback at all. There is no tiredness in the other party, no awkward silence, no look that says “you are overdoing it”. All the information that you are working too long or in a bad state has to come from you. Which is why, in a longer session, it is worth standing up once, leaving the room and coming back — if on your return you cannot remember what you were there for, you were not the one leading.

Practice

A seven-day experiment: the AI charter

This is not a set of rules and not a declaration of purity. It is a decision written down in advance, so that you do not have to take it again twenty times a day, always in a worse state than you are in now.

Day 1 — what I actually use it for. Write out specific uses, not categories. Not “for writing”, but “for the first draft of proposals, which I rewrite anyway”. Not “for learning”, but “for explaining passages I do not understand in texts I am reading anyway”. It usually comes to between five and eight entries — and that alone can be a discovery, because most people use AI for three things and believe they use it for thirty.

Day 2 — what AI does not do in my place. The heart of the charter. Examples worth considering: it does not write messages to people I care about · it does not take decisions whose consequences I will bear · it does not judge whether something is good — at most it says why it might not be · it does not replace first contact with a difficult text · it does not hold a conversation I ought to have with a person · it does not fill the silence in which I was going to think. Your list will be different. What matters is that it is spoken — an unspoken boundary does not work.

Day 3 — the delegation threshold. One sentence, concrete enough to be checked: “the last twenty per cent of anything under my name I do by hand”, or “anything somebody will read and take as a promise, I rewrite from scratch”.

Days 4–7 — the test in motion. The charter is in force. Three sentences of your own before every question to the model. At the end of the day, one word: author or material — which you were in that session.

Day 7 — the exit clause. One question: did this tool strengthen my agency this week, or take it over? If the answer is unclear, it took it over — clarity here is a symptom, not the result of reflection. Then correct one entry in the charter and set it aside for review in a quarter.

What the charter does not promise. It will not protect you from distraction, it is not a form of purification, and it has nothing to do with how many hours you spend at a screen. It does one thing — it keeps authorship with you.

Limits

This chapter has one obvious abuse and it is better to name it outright: refusing to use a tool is sometimes a pose rather than a principle. You can recognise it by a few signs.

By the fact that it gets narrated. A principle works in silence; a pose needs an audience — the announcement “I don’t use that”, dropped in where nobody asked. By the fact that it has no cost: a real boundary costs something, a pose usually costs nothing, because it concerns things you would not have done anyway. By the fact that its price lands on other people — if your “I don’t use it” means a colleague sits three hours longer over something the tool would have done in ten minutes, that is not your purity, it is somebody else’s overtime. And by the fact that it becomes an identity. Someone who has built themselves on the rejection of AI then has to go on rejecting it even where it would be good — otherwise they lose not a tool but the figure they were.

Symmetrically: this method does not work as proof of superiority over those who use things differently. The digital detox as a form of virtue, the flip phone as a status marker, contempt for “the generators” — that is the same game for position, played in the opposite colour.

And two limits of the charter itself. It will not work against external compulsion — if an employer requires particular tools, that is a matter for a conversation or for a decision about the job, not for a private rule. And it does not replace competence: no declaration protects you from accepting a wrong answer in a field you do not know well enough to spot the error.

Ethical question

Four questions that cannot be delegated.

Authorship and disclosure. The point is not to report on every email how it came about. The point is the difference between help with execution and misleading somebody about who was present. A message of condolence, a letter to a friend, feedback for an employee, an apology — these are places where the value lies in the very fact that you gave them your time. Generated, they stop being what they were meant to be, even if they read better.

Whose cost. Text is produced today in a second and read at the same speed as ever. Sending somebody four pages that cost you a minute is shifting your work onto their attention. The rule of fairness is simple: shorten what you send people in proportion to how cheaply it came to you.

Power through the tool. A model is very good at helping to formulate what somebody wants to hear. That is the same capacity that helps you explain and that helps you manipulate — the difference is made solely by whether the other side, knowing everything you know, would still want the conversation. Separately: automating decisions about people — recruitment, assessment, order of service — transfers somebody’s criteria onto other people’s lives, and requires that somebody actually answer for the outcome.

What should stay human. That is the right question and there is no general answer to it. Our proposal: what should stay human is whatever derives its value from presence — the difficult conversation, first contact with a problem, the judgement of whom this will harm, and the decision whether to do it at all. The rest is negotiable and may be handed over without guilt.

The more a tool can do in your place, the more precisely you have to know what you do not want to hand over to it.

Closing formula

There will be no return to a world without these tools, and there is no point pretending that is an option on the table. Nor is there any point in the opposite pose — waiting piously for the systems to decide for us what is worth doing.

What remains is a third road, less striking than either: use a great deal and choose narrowly. Give the machine everything that is execution, and give it nothing that is choice. Keep the intention on your side of the table, even when — especially when — the tool is good enough to tempt you to stop.

Because the statue will go on working whether or not anyone is still in the temple. Your task is not to smash it. Your task is to stay inside.

The tool is not the enemy. The enemy is the moment you stop noticing whose intention it is carrying out.
