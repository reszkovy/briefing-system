## 1. Who Thoth was — FACT

- **Name.** Egyptian **ḏḥwty (Djehuty)**, conventionally vocalized "Thoth" via the Greek Θώθ. The most widely repeated etymology derives ḏḥwty from *ḏḥw*, an old word for the ibis: per Theodor Hopfner's classic analysis, the -ty ending marks possession of the bird's attributes — "he who is like the ibis." The etymology is plausible but not certain (see §6).
  Source: Wikipedia, "Thoth" (citing Hopfner, *Der Tierkult der alten Ägypter*, 1913), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoth (accessed 2026-08-14); Martin A. Stadler, "Thoth," in J. Dieleman & W. Wendrich (eds.), *UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology* (2012), https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2xj8c3qg (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Core portfolio.** Stadler's UCLA Encyclopedia entry — the best concise scholarly overview, open access — characterizes Thoth as "a god of writing and wisdom, a lunar deity, and vizier of the gods," while noting he was also a cosmic deity, creator god, and even warrior in some traditions. He is the divine inventor of hieroglyphic writing, patron of scribes, measurer and divider of time (months, seasons, years — one Egyptian month bore his name, and the first month of the civil year was later called Thoth), and the gods' secretary, messenger, and arbiter.
  Source: Stadler, "Thoth," UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012), https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2xj8c3qg (accessed 2026-08-14); EBSCO Research Starters, "Thoth (deity)," https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/thoth-deity (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Antiquity.** Thoth is attested from the early periods of Egyptian religion and remained continuously important for roughly three millennia, with his animal cult peaking in the Late and Ptolemaic Periods (see §4). Amenhotep III (14th c. BCE) installed colossal baboon statues of Thoth at Hermopolis — still standing on site — an index of state-level cult already in the New Kingdom.
  Source: EBSCO Research Starters, "Thoth (deity)," https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/thoth-deity (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 2. Mythological roles — FACT

- **Weighing of the heart.** In the judgment scene of the *Book of the Dead* (the vignette accompanying spell 125), the deceased's heart is weighed against the feather of Maat; **Thoth stands at the balance as recorder**, writing down the verdict with his scribal palette — the canonical image of the "scribe of the gods" title in action. Anubis handles the scale; Thoth certifies the result.
  Source: Wikipedia, "Thoth" (funerary role, BD 125), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoth (accessed 2026-08-14); EBSCO Research Starters, "Thoth (deity)," https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/thoth-deity (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **The five epagomenal days.** In the version transmitted by Plutarch (*De Iside et Osiride* 12), Thoth (Greek: Hermes) wins light from the moon at draughts and creates the five days outside the 360-day year, allowing Nut to give birth to Osiris, Isis, Seth, and Nephthys despite Ra's curse — the myth that welds Thoth to the calendar.
  Source: Wikipedia, "Thoth" (citing Plutarch), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoth (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Arbiter and healer.** Thoth mediates the contendings of Horus and Seth and heals the wounded Eye of Horus (the *wedjat*); in the Eye-of-Ra cycle he is the god sent to pacify and bring back the raging solar eye. He also serves as vizier/secretary to Osiris and to Ra.
  Source: Stadler, "Thoth," UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012), https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2xj8c3qg (accessed 2026-08-14); EBSCO Research Starters, https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/thoth-deity (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 3. Hermopolis and the cult — FACT

- **Khemenu / Hermopolis Magna.** Thoth's principal cult center was **Khemenu** ("Eight-Town," after the Ogdoad of eight primordial deities) in Middle Egypt — renamed **Hermopolis** ("city of Hermes") by the Greeks, precisely because they equated its god with Hermes. Thoth's standing epithet **nb Ḫmnw, "lord of Khemenu,"** ties him to the site; the local temple complex functioned as a major center of scribal training and priestly scholarship (the "House of Life" institution).
  Source: Stadler, "Thoth," UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012), https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2xj8c3qg (accessed 2026-08-14); EBSCO Research Starters, https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/thoth-deity (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **"Scribe of the gods."** Egyptian scribes regarded Thoth as their divine patron; the scribal statue type shows officials seated beneath Thoth's baboon, and libations to Thoth before writing are attested in scribal culture. His standard titles include scribe (sš) of the Ennead / of the gods and "lord of divine words" (nb mdw-nṯr) — "divine words" being the Egyptian name for hieroglyphs.
  Source: Stadler, "Thoth," UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012), https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2xj8c3qg (accessed 2026-08-14); The Collector, "Who Is the Egyptian God Thoth?," https://www.thecollector.com/who-is-the-egyptian-god-thoth/ (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 4. Ibis and baboon — FACT

- **Two sacred animals.** Thoth appears as an **ibis-headed man**, as the **sacred ibis** itself, and as a **baboon** (Papio hamadryas) — often shown squatting, sometimes with a lunar disk and crescent. The baboon form is old (Early Dynastic baboon figures were later assimilated to Thoth); the ibis's curved beak was associated with both the crescent moon and the scribe's pen in later interpretation.
  Source: Stadler, "Thoth," UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012), https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2xj8c3qg (accessed 2026-08-14); EBSCO Research Starters, https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/thoth-deity (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **The Late Period animal cult was industrial in scale.** From roughly the 7th–4th centuries BCE into Roman times, **millions of ibises were bred, killed, mummified, and dedicated to Thoth** as votive offerings, buried in vast galleries at **Tuna el-Gebel** (the necropolis of Hermopolis) and at **Saqqara** (the Sacred Animal Necropolis of North Saqqara, serving Memphis). Baboons were interred in the same complexes. In 2020 Egyptian archaeologists announced 20 sarcophagi and coffins of members of Thoth's priesthood at Tuna el-Gebel.
  Source: Wikipedia, "Thoth" (Late Period ibis mummification; Tuna el-Gebel 2020 find), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoth (accessed 2026-08-14); EBSCO Research Starters (Tuna el-Gebel, Saqqara, High Priest Petosiris), https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/religion-and-philosophy/thoth-deity (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **The ibis galleries produced the key epithet evidence.** The demotic archive of **Hor of Sebennytos** — ostraca of an ibis-cult functionary at Saqqara, 2nd century BCE, edited by **J. D. Ray, *The Archive of Ḥor* (Egypt Exploration Society, 1976)** — documents the administration of the ibis cult and contains the "three times great" form of Thoth's epithet (see §5).
  Source: Copenhaver, *Hermetica* (Cambridge UP, 1992), Introduction (citing Ray's Archive of Hor and the 172 BCE ibis-cult council minutes), as summarized at Wikipedia, "Hermes Trismegistus," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_Trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 5. Epithets and the road to "Trismegistus" — FACT

This is the pivotal chain for a Hermeticism site, so the epigraphy matters:

- **Step 1 — "the twice great" (ꜥꜣ ꜥꜣ, "great, great").** From the New Kingdom onward, and commonly in Ptolemaic temple inscriptions, Thoth carries the doubled epithet *pꜣ ꜥꜣ ꜥꜣ*, "the great-great" = "twice great / greatest." This is a normal Egyptian intensifying repetition.
  Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Hermes Trismegistus" (development from Egyptian *aa aa*, "great, great," in late hieroglyphic epithets of Thoth), https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/philosophy-and-religion/ancient-religion-biographies/hermes-trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Step 2 — "the great, the great, the great" (tripled).** The often-quoted hieroglyphic instance of the **tripled** epithet is from the **temple of Esna**: "Thoth the great, the great, the great" — the reference popularized in English by **George Hart, *The Routledge Dictionary of Egyptian Gods and Goddesses***, and repeated across the literature. Ptolemaic-era inscriptions attest the *ꜥꜣ ꜥꜣ ꜥꜣ* form from the 3rd century BCE onward.
  Source: Wikipedia, "Hermes Trismegistus" (citing Hart on the Esna epithet), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_Trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Step 3 — demotic "three times great."** In **demotic** documents of the ibis cult at Saqqara — the **Archive of Hor** (2nd c. BCE; Ray 1976) and, per **Brian Copenhaver**, the minutes of an ibis-cult council meeting of **172 BCE** near Memphis — Thoth is called **"the three times great"**, the direct Egyptian-language ancestor of the Greek superlative. Copenhaver treats this as the earliest datable equivalent of "Trismegistos."
  Source: Copenhaver, *Hermetica* (1992), Introduction, via Wikipedia, "Hermes Trismegistus," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_Trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14); Encyclopedia.com, "Hermes Trismegistus" (demotic "five times very great" also attested — the epithet could keep inflating), https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/philosophy-and-religion/ancient-religion-biographies/hermes-trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Step 4 — Greek "Trismegistos."** In Greek, early Ptolemaic dedications call the god of Hermopolis "Hermes the greatest and greatest great god" (doubled μέγιστος forms) before the fused compound appears. Per **Garth Fowden** (*The Egyptian Hermes*, 1986), the first **datable literary occurrences of "Trismegistos"** are in **Athenagoras of Athens** (*Legatio*, 2nd c. CE) and a fragment of **Philo of Byblos** (c. 64–141 CE). So: an Egyptian priestly epithet, translated and compounded in Greek — not a title invented by Greek philosophers.
  Source: Fowden, *The Egyptian Hermes* (Cambridge UP, 1986), via Wikipedia, "Hermes Trismegistus," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_Trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 6. The demotic Book of Thoth — FACT

- **What it is.** A long demotic composition, reconstructed from **over forty Graeco-Roman-period papyri** (1st c. BCE – 2nd c. CE; many from **Tebtunis** and Dime/Soknopaiou Nesos), edited by **Richard Jasnow and Karl-Theodor Zauzich** as *The Ancient Egyptian Book of Thoth: A Demotic Discourse on Knowledge and Pendant to the Classical Hermetica* (Harrassowitz, 2005; revised edition 2021; popular translation *Conversations in the House of Life*, 2014). It is cast as a **dialogue between a master figure — "He-who-praises-knowledge" (probably Thoth or a priest speaking as Thoth) — and a disciple, "He-who-loves-knowledge,"** covering the scribal craft, sacred geography, the underworld, and temple lore.
  Source: Bryn Mawr Classical Review of Jasnow & Zauzich, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2006/2006.05.19/ (accessed 2026-08-14); publisher/edition data, https://www.amazon.com/Ancient-Egyptian-Book-Thoth-Transliteration/dp/3447117176 (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Why it matters for Hermeticism.** Jasnow and Zauzich subtitled the work "Pendant to the Classical Hermetica" because its form — a master-disciple initiatory dialogue on divine knowledge, tied to the temple scriptorium (the **House of Life**) — is the closest known **Egyptian-language structural parallel** to the Greek Hermetic dialogues (Hermes teaching Tat/Asclepius). It is the strongest single piece of evidence that the Hermetica's dialogue form has native Egyptian priestly roots, not just Greek school convention.
  Source: BMCR review, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2006/2006.05.19/ (accessed 2026-08-14); cf. Bull discussion at https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2020/2020.02.23/ (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Note:** this ancient demotic text has **nothing to do** with the "Book of Thoth" of occult legend (a magical book in the fictional Setne cycle) or with Aleister Crowley's 1944 tarot book of the same name — three distinct things sharing one name (see §9).

## 7. Thoth → Hermes Trismegistus (interpretatio graeca) — FACT

- **The equation.** Greeks in Egypt identified Thoth with **Hermes** — both divine messengers, mediators, escorts of the dead (Hermes psychopompos), and patrons of speech and cleverness. The identification predates the Ptolemies: **Plato, *Phaedrus* 274c–275b** (c. 370 BCE), already tells the Egyptian story of **"Theuth"**, inventor of "number and calculation, geometry and astronomy… and above all letters," whose sacred bird is the ibis, debating king Thamus about whether writing is a "remedy (pharmakon) for memory" — the most famous appearance of Thoth in Greek literature. (Plato's *Philebus* 18b also credits Theuth with analyzing speech into letters.)
  Source: Plato, *Phaedrus* 274c ff., Perseus Digital Library, https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0174:text%3DPhaedrus:page%3D274 (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **From god to sage-author.** Under the Ptolemies the fused figure "Hermes" of Hermopolis absorbed Thoth's epithets (§5) and his reputation as author of sacred books. In Greco-Egyptian literature this Hermes was progressively reimagined as a primordial **sage** — a man who attained divine knowledge — to whom astrological, alchemical, magical, and finally philosophical writings (the Hermetica) were attributed. **Fowden** (*The Egyptian Hermes*, 1986) and **Christian H. Bull** (*The Tradition of Hermes Trismegistus*, Brill 2018) trace this trajectory through the bilingual priestly milieu; Bull argues the "inventor of writing become teacher of wisdom" role was carried by actual Egyptian priests into Greek.
  Source: Bryn Mawr Classical Review of Bull, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2020/2020.02.23/ (accessed 2026-08-14); Wikipedia, "Hermes Trismegistus," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_Trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Continuity of place.** Hermopolis and its necropolis Tuna el-Gebel remained the cult's anchor into the Roman period; Greek dedications to "greatest Hermes" from the region are dedications to Thoth. The Greek city name itself is the interpretatio graeca fossilized in geography.
  Source: Stadler, "Thoth," UCLA Encyclopedia of Egyptology (2012), https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2xj8c3qg (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 8. SCHOLARLY DEBATE

- **Etymology of ḏḥwty.** Hopfner's "he of the ibis" derivation is standard but contested; alternative proposals connect the name to words for "messenger" or leave it unexplained. Stadler (UCLA EE, 2012) reviews the options without declaring a winner. Popular sites state the ibis etymology as certain; specialists do not.
  Source: Stadler, "Thoth," https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2xj8c3qg (accessed 2026-08-14); Wikipedia, "Thoth," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thoth (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **When and where "Trismegistos" first appears.** **Fowden** places the first datable Greek occurrences in the 2nd century CE (Athenagoras; Philo of Byblos); **Copenhaver** pushes the functional origin back to the demotic "three times great" of 172 BCE (ibis-cult minutes, Memphis area) and the Archive of Hor. The dispute is really about what counts: the Egyptian epithet chain is continuous, but the Greek compound word itself surfaces late. Both sides are compatible; popularizations that say "the Greeks invented the title" are wrong on the Egyptian evidence.
  Source: Wikipedia, "Hermes Trismegistus" (Fowden vs. Copenhaver datings), https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_Trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **How much of the Hermetica is "really Thoth"?** The maximalist line (**Mahé, Fowden, Bull**; now buttressed by **Jasnow & Zauzich's** Book of Thoth) sees genuine continuity from Egyptian priestly literature to the Greek Hermetica; the older minimalist line (**Festugière**, *La Révélation d'Hermès Trismégiste*, 1944–54) saw only Greek philosophy in Egyptian costume. The Book of Thoth is the main new battlefield: reviewers agree it is a genuine priestly knowledge-dialogue, but how directly it "feeds" the Greek Hermetica remains debated (Jasnow & Zauzich themselves are careful: "pendant," not "source").
  Source: BMCR reviews, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2006/2006.05.19/ and https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2020/2020.02.23/ (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Reading the epithet inflation.** Whether "twice great" → "three times great" → (demotic) "five times great" reflects theological escalation, local rivalry, or simple scribal superlative habit is discussed but unresolved; the "five times very great" demotic form shows the tripling was not a fixed dogmatic formula.
  Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Hermes Trismegistus," https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/philosophy-and-religion/ancient-religion-biographies/hermes-trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14).

## 9. RECEPTION & MYTH

- **Myth: "Thoth was an Atlantean priest-king who built the Great Pyramid."** This is the invention of **"Maurice Doreal"** (Claude Doggins), founder of the Brotherhood of the White Temple (1930), whose *Emerald Tablets of Thoth-the-Atlantean* (c. 1939) claims to translate 36,000-year-old tablets Doreal said he recovered from a pyramid. Scholarship identifies its actual sources as the medieval *Emerald Tablet* legend, Theosophy, and **H. P. Lovecraft's pulp fiction** (notably "The Dunwich Horror"). No Egyptologist, papyrus, or artifact supports any of it — yet Doreal's text is the root of most "Thoth = Atlantis" content online.
  Source: Wikipedia, "Emerald Tablets of Thoth the Atlantean," https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerald_Tablets_of_Thoth_the_Atlantean (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Myth: "The Book of Thoth is a lost book of ultimate magic."** The magical "Book of Thoth" that kills its readers is a plot device in the demotic **Setne Khamwas fiction cycle** (a literary tale, and a cautionary one — the stolen book brings ruin). The real, excavated demotic Book of Thoth (§6) is a scribal-initiation dialogue, not a grimoire. Crowley's *The Book of Thoth* (1944) is a modern tarot treatise borrowing the prestige of the name; the association of tarot with Thoth begins with **Court de Gébelin (1781)** and has no ancient basis.
  Source: Yale Peabody, "Fragments of the Book of Thoth" (Egyptosophy exhibit), https://echoesofegypt.peabody.yale.edu/egyptosophy/fragments-book-thoth (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Distortion: "Thoth wrote 36,525 books."** Late antique authors (Iamblichus citing Seleucus and Manetho) give wildly inflated counts of Hermes' books (20,000 or 36,525 — the number of days in a Sothic-cycle century, i.e. a symbolic figure); Clement of Alexandria more soberly describes 42 canonical Hermetic priestly books. These are claims about a literary persona, routinely misquoted online as a library statistic.
  Source: Encyclopedia.com, "Hermes Trismegistus," https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/philosophy-and-religion/ancient-religion-biographies/hermes-trismegistus (accessed 2026-08-14).
- **Half-truth: "The Greeks stole Thoth."** The interpretatio graeca was a two-way, centuries-long negotiation conducted largely by bilingual Egyptian priests (Bull 2018), not a colonial rebranding event; the Hermetic Hermes keeps Egyptian traits (ibis, Hermopolis, epithet, book-authorship) precisely because Egyptians helped build him.
  Source: BMCR review of Bull, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2020/2020.02.23/ (accessed 2026-08-14).
