The Modern Hermet

TL;DR

A person learning to read the world again. Not a guru, not a cosplay occultist, not an anti-technology survivalist — a reader, an observer, a practitioner of attention, a translator of symbols. This is what the figure means, what the tradition actually hands you, and the small weekly discipline that carries it.

The word needs rescuing. Say hermeticist and most people picture robes, sigils and a man who has read one book about Atlantis. That figure is a costume — and costumes are exactly what this tradition does not need another of.

Here is a better one. A modern hermet is a person learning to read the world again: someone who wants to live more deeply in an age engineered to flatten experience. Not by leaving that age — by paying a different kind of attention inside it.

What it is not

Not an occultist in cosplay. Not a guru, and not anyone’s student-for-a-fee. Not an anti-technology survivalist. Not a collector of “ancient wisdom” quotes with no idea which century they come from. Not a spiritual influencer, because the whole point is a practice that does not need an audience.

What it is

A reader — slowly, with sources. An observer, which is a skill and not a mood. A practitioner of attention: the one capacity everything else depends on. A translator of symbols, able to hear what an image was doing before it became decoration. A person of the threshold — comfortable at the edge between what is known and what is not, without rushing to fill the gap with invention. Someone, in short, looking for depth without an escape hatch from the present.

Eight commitments hold that together, and they are worth stating flatly: attention over distraction · depth over content · symbol over spectacle · practice over performance · mystery with clarity · tradition without cosplay · technology without worship · inner life without escapism.

Two companion pages carry the practice: The Instruction — fifteen practices, from attention to alignment; and Operational Alchemy — twelve principles that turn attention into agency.

What you are actually inheriting

Strip away the costume and the Hermetic texts hand you four things that still hold. They are worth naming plainly, because everything else on this site is a footnote to them.

One: the world is connected, and connection can be read. The doctrine of correspondence — the intuition behind “as above, so below” — is not a claim that stars push people around. It is a habit of attention: assuming that patterns rhyme across scales, and looking for the rhyme. Practised carelessly it becomes superstition. Practised carefully it is close to what we call insight.

Two: knowing can change the knower. The texts call it gnosis, and they are emphatic that its opposite is not ignorance but sleep, drunkenness, forgetting. This is a strong claim and a testable one, in the only laboratory available: you notice, over months, whether what you read has altered what you do.

Three: you are a double being. Mortal by body, something else by essence — the Asclepius calls the human a great miracle, and the ancient authors mean it structurally, not sentimentally: we are the one creature standing on two floors at once. Whatever you make of the metaphysics, it is a serious answer to a question the present is bad at: what a person is for.

Four: there is a route, and it has stations. The Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth is not a poem about ascent; it is a ritual script, with silence, breath and hymn in a fixed order. Someone practised it, together, and wrote down the choreography. That is the ancient world telling us that reading alone was never the whole of it.

A week that holds it

Here is the part most sites either skip or inflate. We will not promise transformation in seven days — but the tradition is not only theory, and pretending otherwise would be its own distortion. What follows is small, ordinary, and drawn from what the sources actually describe.

Read one text slowly, once a week. Fifteen minutes, the same time each week, one section — not a feed. The Hermetica were written to be read this way: the Corpus is a set of teaching dialogues, and dialogues reward re-reading more than speed. The Path is built as exactly this rhythm.

Carry one sentence. Take a single line — “what is below is like what is above”, or Poimandres’ “let the one who has mind recognise himself” — and keep it for the week. Not as a mantra to repeat, but as a lens to look through. Notice where it fits and, more usefully, where it does not.

Keep a page. The Hermetic dialogues are transcripts of someone thinking under questioning. Writing is the nearest modern equivalent to having a teacher: two or three sentences a day about what you actually saw, not what you should have felt.

Practise the pause. The Eighth-and-Ninth text stages silence before speech — deliberately, as a condition of seeing. A minute before the day starts, without a screen, is not a spiritual achievement. It is simply the setting in which the other three become possible.

That is the whole discipline: one text, one sentence, one page, one pause. It fits inside a normal life, which is the only kind of life this is for.

What this asks of you — and what it doesn’t

It does not ask belief. You can hold every metaphysical claim in the Hermetica at arm’s length and still read them the way you would read Plato or the Upanishads: as serious attempts to describe experience, made by people who were not stupid.

It does not ask initiation. There is no organisation here, nothing to join, nobody to become a student of. If a modern teacher tells you the tradition requires their mediation, they are describing their business model, not the texts.

It does not ask you to leave the world. The hermetic move is not escape; the ascent in these texts always returns. Whatever this is, it happens in a life with a job, a phone and Tuesday in it.

What it does ask is patience, and a tolerance for not knowing. Half the interesting questions in this field are open, and the honest posture is to say so.

How to tell sense from nonsense

You will meet a great deal of confident invention. Four questions filter most of it, and they cost nothing:

Where is this from, and when? A real claim can name a text, a date and an edition. “Ancient Egyptian wisdom” is not a source. Is the date checkable? Much of what is sold as immemorial is Victorian or later — the Kybalion is from 1908, the Egyptian tarot from 1781. Does it survive translation? Ideas that only work in vague language usually only work in vague language. What would count as being wrong? A teaching that explains every outcome equally well has stopped explaining anything.

None of these are hostile questions. They are the same ones the best scholars in this field ask, and asking them is not a lack of imagination — it is what keeps imagination worth having.

Where to begin

If you want the orientation first, Start Here takes about ten minutes. If you would rather begin with a real text, go straight to Poimandres — the vision that opens the Corpus, guided line by line in step four of the Path. If you prefer the route walked for you, one step a week, that is what the letter is.

And if you take nothing else: read slowly, check the dates, keep the pause. The rest of this tradition has waited two thousand years. It can wait for you to get there properly.

Fair questions

Why 'hermet' and not 'hermeticist'?

Because the longer word arrives carrying two centuries of costume with it. 'Modern hermet' names a posture rather than a membership: someone who reads, watches and practises attention. If you prefer the older word, use it — nothing here depends on the label.

Am I a hermeticist if I read this?

That is your word to use or not. Nothing here confers a status, and nobody on this site is in a position to confirm one. The practice is the reading; the label is optional and, honestly, mostly gets in the way.

Isn't practice without belief just dilettantism?

We would call it honesty. You can take the attention practices seriously — slow reading, the carried sentence, the pause — without asserting cosmological claims you have no way to test. Many scholars of this material do exactly that.

Why no rituals, invocations or magic instructions?

Because we do history of ideas, and instructing people in ritual would be a different job with different responsibilities. We will tell you what the ancient practitioners did, with sources — the Eighth-and-Ninth text is remarkably specific — and leave what you do with that to you.

Do I need to read in order?

No, but the order helps. The Path is built so each step assumes the last; jumping in works better once you know roughly where the pieces sit, which is what Start Here is for.
