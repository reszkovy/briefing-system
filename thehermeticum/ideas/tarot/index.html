Tarot:
the invented pedigree,
and the real one

TL;DR

Tarot is a fifteenth-century northern Italian trick-taking game. Its Egyptian origin story was invented in 1781 by Antoine Court de Gébelin, four decades before hieroglyphs could be read. The authentic Hermetic connection is later and traceable: Éliphas Lévi, then the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, mapped the trumps onto Kabbalah and astrology — and the whole system rests on the genuinely ancient idea of correspondence.

Ask where tarot comes from and you will be told: ancient Egypt, the priests of Thoth, wisdom smuggled through the centuries in pictures. It is a wonderful story. It is also, in every checkable particular, false — and the true story is better, because we can name the man who invented the legend and the year he published it.

What the cards actually were

Tarot appears in northern Italy in the middle of the fifteenth century — Milan, Ferrara, Bologna — as a game. The early name is trionfi, later tarocchi: a trick-taking card game with a fifth suit of permanent trumps. The oldest surviving decks were painted for the Visconti and Sforza courts, luxury objects for players, not oracles. For roughly three hundred years the historical record shows people doing one thing with these cards: gambling.

The invention of Egypt

The Egyptian origin has an author and a date. In 1781 the Swiss pastor and scholar Antoine Court de Gébelin devoted a chapter of his encyclopaedic Monde primitif to the claim that tarot preserved the lost Book of Thoth, Egyptian priestly doctrine hidden in plain sight. He offered no evidence, and he could not have had any: he wrote four decades before Champollion deciphered hieroglyphs, at a moment when “Egyptian” functioned in French letters as a synonym for primordial wisdom. Within a few years the cartomancer Etteilla had turned the idea into a business — the first decks designed for divination rather than play.

So the “ancient Egyptian tarot” is younger than the steam engine.

Where the Hermetic connection is real

Here honesty cuts the other way. In the nineteenth century Éliphas Lévi mapped the twenty-two trumps onto the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet, welding tarot to Kabbalah. The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn — the name is not decoration — systematised the whole thing from 1888: trumps assigned to the paths of the Tree of Life, suits to elements, cards to planets and signs. Out of that workshop came the two decks that define the modern imagination of tarot: Waite–Smith (1909) and Crowley and Harris’s Thoth (1944).

That lineage is genuinely Hermetic — but it is the Hermeticism of the Renaissance revival and the occult orders, not of second-century Alexandria. Nothing in the Corpus Hermeticum, the Asclepius or the Emerald Tablet knows anything about cards, for the excellent reason that playing cards did not reach Europe until the 1370s.

The deeper link, which is a real one

Strip away the fake pedigree and something substantial remains. A tarot system works — as a symbolic machine, whatever you think it does or doesn’t reveal — only on the assumption that the levels of reality answer one another: that a card can stand for a planet, a planet for a temperament, a temperament for a moment in a life. That assumption is correspondence, and it is authentically ancient. In that sense tarot is not a Hermetic relic. It is a late, practical child of Hermetic cosmology — which is a stronger claim than the Egyptian one, and true.

One detail almost nobody knows

Playing cards did come to Europe from Egypt. Mamluk decks — suits of cups, coins, swords and polo-sticks — reached Italy and Spain around the 1370s, and our suits descend from them. Court de Gébelin was pointing at the right country and the wrong thing: the medium is Egyptian, the mysticism is French and eighteenth-century.

Fair questions

Is tarot ancient Egyptian?

No. The cards are fifteenth-century Italian and the Egyptian story dates from 1781. Court de Gébelin wrote before hieroglyphs could be read, so no one could have checked his claim — and no evidence has appeared since.

Is tarot Hermetic, then?

In the modern sense, yes — genuinely so. The Golden Dawn built a full Hermetic-Kabbalistic system onto the deck from 1888, and the standard decks descend from that work. In the ancient sense, no: the Hermetica predate playing cards in Europe by more than a thousand years.

Do you read tarot on this site?

No. We do history of ideas, not divination — that rule applies here as everywhere. What we can offer is the real story of where the symbols came from, which is more interesting than most readings.

Why does the Egyptian myth survive?

Because it is a better story than 'Milanese card game', because Etteilla had a product to sell, and because the Golden Dawn's genuinely sophisticated system gave the legend intellectual cover it never earned.
